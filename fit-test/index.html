More tests before printing

  1. Layer-line preview in the browser (Kotlin): Slice the mesh at each layer height and draw the outlines as stacked lines in three.js with a layer-height slider; shows stair-stepping on the thread, bend and fillet, live, no slicer needed. Effort: ½–1 day.
  2. Real slicer G-code preview: Slice with OrcaSlicer or CuraEngine on the command line and draw the toolpaths in three.js; shows true walls, infill and supports plus print time and grams. Effort: 1–2 days.
  3. “As printed” fit test: Round the cap and head to layer height and 0.4 mm line width, then rerun the screw-down sweep; the M9×1.25 thread gets only ~8–10 layers per pitch at 0.12–0.16 mm. Effort: ~1 day.
  4. Overhang / support heatmap: Colour every surface steeper than 45° red to show where the floating neck needs support and confirm the threads print without it. Effort: ½ day.
  5. Layer direction check: Flag anywhere layer lines cross the neck; layer bonding is only ~30–50 % as strong as along the lines. Effort: a few hours.
  6. Thin-wall check: Measure wall thickness and flag anything under two lines (0.8 mm): collet fingers, thread tips, cap wall. Effort: ~1 day.
  7. Stress simulation (FEA): Mesh the neck, fillet and fingers and simulate brushing load to confirm the 22.5 MPa formula and the real stress at the fillet. Effort: 1–2 days.
  8. Finger flex check: Beam maths: ~0.4 mm tip travel on a 6.5 mm, 1.2 mm thick finger is ~1.9 % strain vs ~4 % for PETG to yield; warn above 2.5 %. Effort: a few hours.
  9. Swollen-stick check: Sweep the stick diameter wet (5.0 → 5.4 mm) against the hex bore and grip. Effort: a few hours.
  10. Bed contact and print time / filament: Bed contact from the mesh; time and grams from the G-code (now ~7 g PETG for the handle). Effort: a few hours after #2.

Ways to make it stronger

  1. Neck Ø4 → Ø5: Strength grows with diameter cubed: stress at 5 N drops from 22.5 to ~11.5 MPa (Ø4.5 gives ~15.8 MPa).
  2. Oval neck, ~4 × 5.5 mm: Long side along the main brushing load: about as strong as Ø5 round while staying 4 mm thin from the other side.
  3. Keep the print orientation, solid neck: Layers already run along the neck, so never print it upright; use 100 % infill or 5+ walls, since walls carry the load in small parts.
  4. Taper the neck to the load: Ideal diameter grows with the cube root of the bending load: keep Ø4 only where the load is smallest and grow toward the bend.
  5. Round the remaining sharp inside corners: The R2.5 fillet is already near its useful limit; the next weak spots are slit ends, hex bore corners and knurl grooves.
  6. Round holes at the collet slit ends (Ø0.8–1.0 mm): Removes the crack starters at the base of the 1.2 mm fingers and spreads the flex.
  7. Friendlier thread: Blunt lead-in on the first turn, flat tips ≥ 0.4 mm with a 0.1–0.15 mm root radius or a 30° trapezoid profile, maybe 1.5 mm pitch; rerun the fit test after.
  8. Shallow cap knurl (0.3–0.4 mm, rounded grooves): The cap wall over the M9 thread is only ~1.7 mm, so keep ≥ 1.2 mm under the knurl.
  9. Material: PETG is the best all-rounder (~50 MPa, fine wet); PLA is brittle and softens at 55–60 °C; nylon is toughest but swells with water; PC is strong but hard to print; resin only in a tough grade.
  10. Web on the inside of the bend: Works, but harder to clean and less slim; an I-beam neck isn't practical at Ø4 (flanges thinner than two print lines).
